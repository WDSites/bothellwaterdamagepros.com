What Does Water Damage Look Like? A Bothell, WA Guide

Stains, bubbles, smells and sounds all mean something. Here is how to spot water damage early, find where it hides and decide what to do first.

Water damage rarely announces itself

People expect water damage to mean a flooded floor. Most of the time it looks smaller. A brown spot, a bubble in the paint, a faint smell in a closet. Learning these signs early is the difference between a repair and a rebuild.

This guide walks room by room through what water damage looks like, smells like and sounds like, and tells you what to do when you find it.

Local note

Freezing nights and Bothell pipes

Weather normals from the Seattle-Tacoma airport show 7.0 days with a low of 32 degrees or colder in January and 7.2 in December, and 25.2 such days in a year. A supply line in an exterior wall or an unheated crawl space can freeze on those nights, split and leak when the temperature rises.

The Washington insurance commissioner says burst pipes, including frozen pipes, are usually covered as long as you heat your home properly. Keep the heat on while you travel in winter.

Source type: Weather-service climate normals and state insurance commissioner.

Signs you can see

Stains

Yellow, tan or brown patches on ceilings and walls, often with a darker ring at the edge.

Bubbling or peeling paint

Moisture pushes paint off the wall from behind. Peeling near baseboards points to water from below.

Warped or cupped floors

Boards lift at the edges or buckle across the room.

Sagging

Ceilings and drywall that droop are holding water.

Rust and corrosion

Rust on pipe fittings, appliance bases and metal trim points to repeated moisture.

Visible mold

Spots in corners, on grout or behind furniture. Mold needs moisture to grow.

Signs you can smell or hear

  • A musty, earthy odor that returns after cleaning.
  • Dripping or running sounds with no fixtures in use.
  • A hissing or rushing noise near a wall or ceiling, which can mean a pressurized leak.
  • Pops and creaks in wood floors as boards swell.
  • A water meter that moves when every tap is off.

The three categories of water

Restoration professionals sort water into three categories by contamination. Category 1 comes from a clean source, such as a supply line. Category 2 is gray water, such as a washing machine overflow. Category 3 is grossly contaminated, such as sewage or flood water.

The category affects what can be saved. The EPA says flood water may have high levels of raw sewage or other hazardous substances, and advises discarding anything that has been wet for more than 24 to 48 hours. If you suspect sewage or flood water, do not handle it without protection.

What to do when you find it

Stop the source

Shut the fixture valve or the main water valve.

Make it safe

If water is near outlets or the panel, stay out and call 911 and your utility.

Document it

Photograph the area before you touch anything.

Call for help

Early drying keeps repairs small. See emergency water removal if water is standing.

Report to insurance

Report a sudden loss to your insurer right away.

How quickly does it get worse

Water that sits in drywall, insulation and flooring does not stay put. The EPA advises drying water-damaged areas and items within 24 to 48 hours to prevent mold growth. After that, damage spreads, finishes fail and costs go up.

A professional uses moisture meters to see damage that the eye cannot. If you suspect a hidden leak, ask for a moisture check before the walls are opened.

What a moisture check involves

A moisture meter has pins or a flat pad that read the water content of a surface. A technician moves across walls, floors and ceilings and notes the readings on a map. Thermal imaging cameras show temperature differences that point to wet material.

These tools do not replace judgment. A reading means something only in comparison with a dry reference from the same material in an unaffected area. Ask the technician to show you both readings.

Small leaks that do big damage

  • A drip from a supply line under a sink can wet the cabinet floor and the wall behind it.
  • A toilet seal that weeps can soak the subfloor without ever puddling.
  • A window or door that leaks only in wind-driven rain can wet the same framing again and again.
  • A refrigerator line with a pinhole can run behind the unit for months.
  • A leaking water heater can wet the floor under a closet or a wall.
  • A gutter that overflows at a corner can feed moisture to a wall for years.

Water also hides in ceilings below upstairs bathrooms and in walls behind tubs. A faint stain on a ceiling is often the first sign of a problem far above it.
