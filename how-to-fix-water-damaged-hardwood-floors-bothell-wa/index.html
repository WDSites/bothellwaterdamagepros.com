How to Fix Water Damaged Hardwood Floors: A Bothell, WA Guide

Cupped, stained or lifted boards do not always mean a new floor. Here is how to read the damage, dry the wood the right way and know what repair makes sense.

What water does to a hardwood floor

Wood is a natural sponge. It takes up water through its edges and underside and swells, and a swollen board pushes against its neighbors. That pressure shows up as cupping, where the edges rise above the center, or crowning, where the center rises above the edges.

In a severe case the floor buckles, lifting away from the subfloor. A cupped floor can sometimes recover. A buckled floor usually cannot.

Local note

Wet season and Bothell floors

The Seattle-Tacoma airport weather normals show 3.91 inches of rain in October, 6.31 in November, 5.72 in December and 5.78 in January. That four-month stretch is when exterior door leaks, window leaks and crawl space moisture are most likely to reach hardwood floors in Bothell.

The city also records that a heavy rainfall from December 1 to 17, 2007 was its most recent significant flood. Storms of that kind put water against foundations for days, which is why crawl space checks matter.

The first day

Stop the water

Shut the source off and soak up standing water with towels and a wet vacuum.

Pull rugs and furniture

Anything on the floor traps moisture and stains the wood.

Get air moving

Fans help. Keep the room warm and use a dehumidifier.

Do not sand or refinish

Sanding wet wood locks in the damage and makes crowning more likely.

Get readings

A moisture meter shows how wet the boards and subfloor are.

How professionals dry a wood floor

Crews use specialized drying mats and air movers that pull moisture out through the wood face, sometimes with small holes drilled to reach trapped water under the boards. Dehumidifiers keep the room dry so moisture does not return to the wood.

Drying can take days, and rushing it causes problems. Boards move as they lose water, and cupping often relaxes during drying. The crew tracks moisture readings until the floor reaches a stable level. Then the floor is judged for repair.

After drying: sand, patch or replace

If boards have flattened and the stains are light, a sanding and refinishing can restore the floor. If a few boards are stained black or permanently warped, they can be cut out and replaced with matching wood, then blended into the finish.

If the damage covers a large part of the room, replacing the whole floor produces a more uniform result. A water damage repair crew can scope each option and explain the price before work starts.

Subfloor and underlayment matter

A hardwood floor is only as good as what is under it. Water that soaks into plywood or sleepers keeps feeding moisture into the wood after the surface dries. The subfloor must read dry before any refinishing.

If the home has a crawl space, the Washington insurance commissioner recommends checking it regularly for dampness, moldy smells, sagging insulation and moisture on the vapor barrier. A wet crawl space can ruin a floor from below.

What not to do

  • Do not put heat guns or space heaters directly on the floor. Fast drying cracks boards.
  • Do not cover the floor with plastic, which traps moisture.
  • Do not wax or seal damp wood.
  • Do not walk on cupped boards with heavy loads.
  • Do not put down new flooring over a damp subfloor.

Insurance and hardwood floors

A sudden leak from a pipe or an appliance is the kind of loss home insurance is meant for. Slow seepage, groundwater and flood are treated differently, and the Washington commissioner says most policies do not cover gradual leaks that go on for long.

Photograph the floor from several angles, record the date and cause, and keep the damaged boards if your adjuster wants to inspect them. Ask your insurer how it treats a floor that can be refinished versus one that must be replaced.

Refinishing basics

Refinishing removes the old finish and a thin layer of wood, then applies stain and a protective coat. It works when the boards are dry and flat enough to sand. It cannot repair rot or black staining that goes deep into the wood.

A professional floor crew can tell you how many sandings the floor has left. Boards that were sanded many times in the past may be too thin to sand again.
