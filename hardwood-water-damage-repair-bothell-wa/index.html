Hardwood water damage repair: the decision, not the technique

Most hardwood articles explain how to dry a wet floor. This one covers the next question, which is what repair is worth doing after the floor is dry. A floor can survive a leak and still need a rebuild, and the choice shapes the cost, the timeline and the look of the room.

The right answer comes from three facts: how far the damage spreads, how thick the wood is and whether the subfloor is sound. A contractor with a moisture meter and a flashlight can tell you all three in an hour.

Local note

Hiring a flooring contractor in Bothell

Washington State requires all construction contractors to register with the Department of Labor and Industries. The registration number must appear on advertising, bids and estimates, so ask for it and check it with the state before signing.

The City of Bothell lists a Building Department and Permit Center under Community Development. The city's pages do not mention floor repairs after water damage specifically, so ask them whether your scope needs a permit.

How far does the damage spread?

Water travels under a floor faster than it shows on top. A leak that wet one corner may have crept under the boards across the room. Moisture mapping with a meter and, where available, a thermal camera shows the real footprint.

A repair that stops at the visible edge of the stain often misses wet wood and fails a year later. Ask the contractor to show you the readings and the boundary.

Choosing boards that match

A replacement board is a different age from the old ones. New wood is lighter, and old wood is darker and sometimes a different species or grade. Contractors reduce the contrast by sanding the whole room to a single tone, then staining and finishing together.

If the old flooring is discontinued, ask the installer to pull boards from a closet or a hidden corner for repairs. This is a common way to get a close match.

Hiring the right people

The Washington Department of Labor and Industries says all construction contractors must register with the state, and the registration number must appear on advertising, bids and estimates. It lists bond and liability insurance requirements for registered contractors.

The state health department adds that there are no specific certification requirements for performing mold or water damage restoration in Washington, so ask for licensing, bonding and insurance. The IICRC offers voluntary certification. Get the scope, price and schedule in writing.

Repair versus replace: a cost lens

  • Total area affected, since price scales with square footage.
  • Wood species and thickness, which determine how many times the floor can be sanded.
  • Subfloor condition, which can add steps before any wood goes down.
  • Finish type, since matching a factory finish is harder than a site-finished one.
  • Whether furniture, trim and baseboards need to come off.

We do not give exact prices without seeing the floor. For a covered loss, document the scope for your insurer and ask how it treats repair versus replacement. Water mitigation comes first, and the rebuild follows once the readings are stable.

Questions to ask before you sign

  • Can you show me your moisture readings for the wood and the subfloor?
  • How will you blend new boards with the old ones?
  • What is the schedule, and which steps need drying time?
  • What does the written estimate include, and what could change it?
  • Do you hold the state contractor registration, and can I verify it?
  • How do you protect the rest of the home from dust?

A realistic timeline

Drying takes days. Board replacement takes a day or two. Sanding and finishing add several more days because each coat of finish needs time to cure, and the room cannot be used while it does. Plan for a week or more of disruption for even a modest repair.

Ask the contractor when furniture can come back. Putting weight on a floor before the finish cures leaves marks that cannot be fixed without refinishing again.

Protecting the repair afterward

A repaired floor needs the same attention as the original. Keep humidity steady, use mats at doors, fix leaks fast and wipe spills. Felt pads under furniture prevent dents.

If the water came from a recurring source, such as a window that leaks in storms, fix that source first. Otherwise the repair is only a delay.
