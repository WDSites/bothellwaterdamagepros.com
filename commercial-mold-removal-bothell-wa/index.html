Commercial mold is a business problem first

When mold turns up in a shop, office, restaurant or apartment building, three things happen at once. A space has to stay open or close. Someone has to find the moisture. Someone has to decide who pays. A residential mindset of scrub and hope does not scale to that.

This guide is for owners, managers and tenants of small commercial and multifamily properties in Bothell. It explains how to think about the job and what to ask a restoration company.

Local note

Canyon Park retail and Downtown multifamily

The Canyon Park Subarea Plan describes retail centers on all four quadrants of the 228th Street SE and SR 527 intersection. The city's comprehensive plan says Canyon Park is the only Regional Growth Center within Bothell designated by the Puget Sound Regional Council, and that most larger multifamily buildings are Downtown.

Both kinds of property share roofs, plumbing and ventilation across many units, which is why a single moisture problem can reach many tenants.

Source type: City subarea plan and comprehensive plan executive summary.

What commercial properties face

Larger affected areas

Commercial rooms are bigger, and the state guidance for professionals starts at about ten square feet.

Shared systems

HVAC, roofs, plumbing risers and ceilings are shared across spaces, so moisture and spores spread.

Business interruption

A closed room means lost revenue. Plan how to keep the rest of the space open safely.

Lease and liability questions

Landlords, tenants and insurers may each have a part. Read the lease.

Safety steps before the crew arrives

  • Keep people out of the affected area and do not disturb visible growth.
  • Turn off fans and HVAC that blow across the area, so spores do not spread.
  • Stop any active water source and keep electrical equipment away from wet areas.
  • Do not demolish walls or ceilings before the scope is written.
  • Send health questions from staff or tenants to their own physicians.

A sequence for managers

Stop the water

No remediation works while moisture continues. Find and fix the source.

Document the conditions

Photograph the areas, record dates and note who reported it.

Notify the right parties

Landlord, tenant, insurer and property manager each need to know.

Define the scope in writing

Ask the contractor to show the affected area, the plan and the clearance step.

Contain and remove

The work area is sealed and filtered so the rest of the building stays clean.

Dry and verify

Moisture readings confirm the area is dry before rebuild.

Standards and licensing

The industry standard for mold remediation is the ANSI/IICRC S520. The Washington Department of Health says there are no specific certification requirements for mold or water damage restoration in Washington State, and advises hiring a contractor who is licensed, bonded and insured. The IICRC offers voluntary certification.

The state also requires construction contractors to register with the Department of Labor and Industries. Ask for the registration number and verify it. For commercial work, ask for proof of insurance as well.

Testing and clearance

Some commercial jobs include air or surface testing before and after remediation. Testing is a decision for the property owner, insurer or an independent consultant. The remediation crew should explain their own verification, such as moisture readings and a visual check.

Be wary of a company that both tests and remediates and has an incentive to find more work. A written scope and a third-party clearance, where your lease or insurer requires one, give you an independent check.

Insurance and leases

The Washington commissioner says many homeowner policies offer limited mold coverage, but only when the mold results from a covered loss. Commercial policies differ and can exclude mold entirely or cap it. Ask your commercial insurer and read the lease before work starts.

In multifamily buildings, ask who owns the wall, the pipe and the roof. The answer usually decides who pays. Document everything, and keep copies of every written notice.

Reducing risk in a commercial space

  • Inspect roofs, flashing and rooftop units before the rainy season.
  • Service HVAC condensate lines and drain pans.
  • Set up a leak-reporting process for tenants and staff.
  • Control humidity in storage rooms, kitchens and bathrooms.
  • Check ceiling tiles for stains after storms.
  • Keep a list of shutoff valve locations posted.

For water events, start with emergency water removal so mold does not get a head start.

Questions to ask a restoration company

  • Will you provide a written scope that names each affected area?
  • How will you contain the work area and protect occupied spaces?
  • What equipment will you use for air filtration and drying?
  • How will you verify that the area is dry and clean?
  • Who disposes of the removed materials, and how?
  • What does the schedule look like, and how will you keep the business open?

Keeping tenants and customers informed

People notice work signs, plastic barriers and equipment noise. A short written notice helps. Say what the work is, where it takes place, how long it should last and who to contact.

Do not speculate about health. If someone raises a health concern, direct them to their own physician. Keep the notice factual, and keep a copy for your records.

Records worth keeping

Keep photographs, dates, moisture readings, notices to tenants, invoices and correspondence with insurers in one place. If a dispute arises over cause or cost, a clear file helps every party. It also helps with the next incident.
