Where Maywood and Beckstrom Hill sit, between two creek valleys

.

Bothell treats Maywood and Beckstrom Hill as one subarea in its city code, and this page does the same. The city's plan describes about 898 acres on most of a north-south hill between the North Creek and Horse Creek valleys, in both the King County and Snohomish County portions of Bothell. Ground runs from roughly 80 to roughly 370 feet.

Routes include 104th Avenue NE, 100th Avenue NE, NE 190th Street and 242nd Street SE. The hill borders Downtown and Canyon Park, so a house near an edge may sit closer to Downtown Bothell or Canyon Park.

The plan says some of Bothell's oldest homes, from the early 1900s, remain here, while most single-family homes are in subdivisions built from the late 1950s and early 1960s. It also describes the Green Acres manufactured home park and a Lower Maywood district where multifamily buildings are allowed.

What the city's plan says about water on the hill

.

Horse Creek drains south from Lake Pleasant, mostly open channel for about a mile and then a 36-inch pipe to the Sammamish River. North Creek runs along the east side. South Fork Perry Creek starts at three drumlin lakes east of Green Acres, where stormwater collects.

Wetlands lie north of Lake Pleasant and along 19th Avenue SE, and steep, heavily treed slopes in the northeast corner are protected. For a house, that means wet ground after long rain and possible seepage below a slope.

Source type: City of Bothell subarea plans.

Local news reported that the original Horse Creek pipe overflowed in large storms downstream in Downtown, before a new channel opened in 2016. That was Downtown, not here. For Maywood and Beckstrom Hill itself, no local flood history was found in the public sources checked.

Flood maps and flood insurance on the hill

.

Neighborhood-specific FEMA flood-zone status not verified. Mapped zones in Bothell follow stream reaches, so the answer can differ by address. Homeowners can look up their own address on the FEMA Flood Map Service Center.

The December 2007 storm and January 2009 atmospheric river were regional events, not confirmed damage here. Standard homeowners policies usually exclude flood, so ask your insurer.

Water problems that fit these hills

.

Houses from the late 1950s and early 1960s can still have original plumbing or an old tank, though age alone says little about one house. A split line is a burst pipe water damage job, and a failed tank is water heater leak cleanup.

Below the slopes, groundwater can show up under a raised floor, covered on crawl space water damage. In multifamily buildings one leak can wet several units, which is where structural drying matters.

Water damage safety: what to do first

.
  • Stop the source if it is safe to reach, such as a fixture valve or the main.
  • Do not enter flooded areas.
  • Do not operate breakers or touch electrical equipment while standing in water, or if water reached outlets or the panel.
  • Creek water and sewage are contaminated, so no DIY drying or cleanup.
  • Photograph before moving things.
  • Do not assume everything is lost, and do not assume all of it can be rescued.

How a crew works through a wet house

.

Extract

Pumps and extractors remove standing water.

Map the moisture

Meters and a thermal camera show how far water traveled.

Dry

Air movers and dehumidifiers run while readings are logged.

Remove what cannot dry

Soaked padding, insulation and drywall are cut out.

Document

Photos, readings and a list of removed materials are kept.

If materials stay wet, mold can grow, so drying quickly matters. See mold remediation for that question.

Documenting a loss for your insurer

.

Keep dated photos, receipts, moisture readings and notes on what was removed. Ask your insurer what applies to your policy and what it wants to see.
