Kirkland homes by decade, and what each decade leaks

Water damage restoration in Kirkland, WA starts with the age of the building, because the age predicts the leak. Dispatch is open 24/7, and I-405 ties Kirkland to our Bothell base, so crews reach every part of the city from the same line.

The city's housing inventory says about 22 percent of Kirkland's homes were built before 1970 and 42 percent between 1970 and 1989. That second group is the biggest. Kingsgate's subdivisions, such as Kingsgate Highlands, High Woodlands and Hazen Hills, were built in the 1960s and 1970s. Houses from those years are now old enough for original water heaters, supply lines and roof flashing to fail, so those are the first places we look.

1960s and 1970s houses

Check the water heater, washing machine hoses, shower valves and the roof edges. Original materials are past the age most manufacturers rate them for.

Newer townhomes

Leaks tend to come from appliance lines, ice makers and upstairs bathrooms. Water drops through ceilings into the unit below.

Juanita multifamily

The city says Juanita has the most multifamily units of any Kirkland neighborhood. Water travels between floors, so we document every unit it reached.

Totem Lake redevelopment

The Totem Lake plan projects housing growing from 5,021 units in 2022 to 9,421 by 2044. New buildings still have plumbing and roof failures during early life.

Hills, creeks and the lakefront

Kirkland averages 38.6 inches of rain a year, according to the city's own profile, and the ground tilts toward Lake Washington. Much of the Forbes Creek valley is mapped as a flood hazard and seismic hazard area. The only 100-year floodplain in the Totem Lake business district is around Totem Lake itself, and the city notes that FEMA maps there may be out of date.

The city's 2018 landslide mapping found the Lake Washington shore and Finn Hill most vulnerable, and its plan calls the South Juanita slope unstable. For a homeowner that means water from the hillside can reach crawl spaces and lower walls long after the rain stops.

King County's 2025 annex lists zero repetitive-loss properties in Kirkland, so formal flood claims are rare. Even so, flood water is Category 3 and a standard policy usually excludes it. Check the FEMA flood map for your address and ask your insurer.

Stormwater trouble spots in Kirkland

Kirkland's surface water master plan describes a Totem Lake Boulevard flooding problem caused by an undersized 12-inch pipe, fixed with a bypass that reroutes about 40 acres of runoff. The city also built storm improvements at NE Juanita Drive and 86th Avenue NE, where water had been flooding the roadway and freezing in winter.

Those projects show the pattern. Heavy rain moves faster over pavement and roofs than old pipes can carry it. If water is entering your property from the street side, report it to the city, then call us about the damage inside.

When water is spreading in Kirkland

Whatever the source, these steps come first. Skip any that are not safe where you stand.

Cut off the water supply

Shut the valve under the fixture or the main shutoff for the house. A water heater leak is stopped at its own cold water valve.

Look at the power

If water has reached outlets, a panel, a lamp or an appliance, stay out. Call 911 and your utility before you call us.

Get valuables up

Put electronics, documents and photo albums on a table or upstairs. Leave heavy furniture where it is.

Keep everyone clear of dirty water

Lakeshore, creek and sewer water can carry bacteria. Keep children and pets away from it.

Photograph everything

Take photos and video of the source, each wet room and each damaged item before cleanup begins.

Water and sewer service differs by Kirkland neighborhood

The city serves most residents with water, but Northshore Utility District and Woodinville Water District provide water to Finn Hill, Kingsgate and North Juanita. For sewer, the city serves the area south of NE 116th Street and Northshore serves most of the area north of it. The city's comprehensive plan also says roughly 5 to 10 percent of residents use septic systems.

Before you call about a backup, find which provider is on your bill. A septic tank problem is not a utility problem at all, and the cleanup differs. Sewage is Category 3 water, and we remove and disinfect whatever it touched, whatever the cause.

Permit rules that matter after a loss

Kirkland's Development Services Center lists drywall removal or installation and water heater replacement among the work that needs a permit. It also says its exemptions do not apply in flood hazard, wetland, steep slope or shoreline areas. The page does not address emergency water damage, so ask the center how the rules apply before walls come down.

Keep the permit question separate from the emergency. Extraction and drying start right away, and a written scope shows what the rebuild will involve.
